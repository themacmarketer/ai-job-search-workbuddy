# Interview Preparation Guide

## STAR Format

Structure answers as: **Situation** (context), **Task** (your responsibility), **Action** (what you did), **Result** (outcome).

Keep answers to 1-2 minutes. Be specific. End with what you learned or would do differently.

## Ready-Made STAR Examples

### 1. Cutting forecast variance from 18% to 7% (analytical rigour, process ownership)
**S:** Sales forecasting across six APAC markets was unreliable. Variance ran at 18%, which meant the region was either over-stocking or missing numbers, and nobody trusted the monthly number enough to plan against it.
**T:** Own the forecast and pipeline review cadence, and make the number trustworthy.
**A:** Rebuilt the review cadence rather than the spreadsheet — changed what each market was accountable for and when, standardised the pipeline definitions so "committed" meant the same thing in six countries, and built the monthly commercial dashboard in Excel and Power BI so variance was visible at market level instead of only in aggregate.
**R:** Variance fell from 18% to 7% over three years — roughly a 60% reduction.
**Use for:** "Tell me about an improvement you made", "How do you handle a metric nobody trusts?", "Give me an example of process design"

### 2. Leading the CRM migration to Salesforce (influencing without authority)
**S:** The region ran on a legacy CRM that could not support the reporting the business needed. A migration touches every salesperson's daily workflow, and the people doing the work had no reason to want it.
**T:** Lead the migration across the region in 2021 without direct authority over the sales teams.
**A:** Sequenced the rollout by market rather than by feature, ran the change alongside the forecast cadence so adoption had a visible purpose, and kept the pipeline definitions consistent between old and new systems so nobody's number moved for a reason they could not explain.
**R:** Completed the migration in 2021. The clean data foundation is what made the later variance reduction measurable at all.
**Use for:** "Tell me about a change you led", "How do you get people to adopt a tool?", "Influencing without authority"

### 3. Twelve key accounts worth SGD 28M a year (commercial negotiation)
**S:** Managed 12 key accounts at Brightwell Industrial Supplies generating SGD 28 million annually, in industrial distribution where margin sits in rebate structures rather than list price.
**T:** Hold and grow the accounts through annual contract cycles.
**A:** Negotiated annual contracts and rebate tiers account by account, matching volume commitments to realistic forecasts rather than aspirational ones — which is the same discipline that later showed up in the forecasting work.
**R:** Retained the book across eight years, and was promoted to Senior Key Account Manager in 2013.
**Use for:** "Tell me about a commercial negotiation", "How do you manage a large account book?", "What does good look like in account management?"

### 4. Scoring 200 leads with generative AI in 40 minutes (learning agility, applied AI)
**S:** Week 4 of the SMU Academy GenAI certificate. Lead scoring is a task I have done manually for years, and I wanted to test whether the tool actually changed the economics of it.
**T:** Score 200 leads against a defined rubric and compare the effort honestly.
**A:** Used ChatGPT with a synthetic dataset and an explicit scoring rubric, keeping the rubric identical to the one I would have applied by hand so the comparison was like for like.
**R:** 40 minutes instead of roughly a day. **The accuracy check against real outcomes is the next step and is not done** — say so if asked; claiming a validated result here would be the one thing that undermines the whole story.
**Use for:** "Tell me about something you have learned recently", "How do you use AI in your work?", "Why the career pivot?"

## Singapore-specific interview topics

<!-- LOCALISED: Singapore. Prepare these alongside the STAR examples — they come up in
     almost every Singapore interview and are not covered by a competency answer. -->

### The four questions that decide the outcome

**1. "What is your current and expected salary?"**
Usually asked in the first screen, often before anything technical. Answer in **monthly
gross SGD**, as a range, with the market data behind it (see `04-job-evaluation.md`
Method A and `08-application-forms.md`). Do not answer in annual terms — it signals you
have not worked in this market.

**2. "What is your notice period?"**
One month is the norm. Answer as a duration. If you are not currently employed, say
`Available immediately` — but be ready for the follow-up about the gap.

**3. "Are you authorised to work in Singapore?"**
Answer with the pass class, factually: `Singapore Citizen`, `PR`, `Employment Pass holder`,
or `I would require Employment Pass sponsorship.` If you need sponsorship, do not soften
it into "I'm sure that can be arranged" — the recruiter needs the fact to assess quota and
COMPASS, and discovering it late wastes everyone's time.

**4. "Why Singapore?"** (for candidates without local status)
Have a specific, non-generic answer: the regional-HQ concentration, a particular sector
cluster (finance, biomedical, logistics), or family/relocation reasons stated plainly.
"We want to explore Asia" fails.

### How Singapore interviews are structured

- **Panel interviews are common**, especially in banking, GLCs, statutory boards and
  larger corporates: three to five people, one of whom says almost nothing and writes
  everything down. Address answers to the whole panel, not just the person who asked.
- **Public sector and GLC processes are slower and more procedural** — expect multiple
  rounds, sometimes a written component, and longer gaps between stages. Do not read
  silence as rejection.
- **Technical assessment is normal in tech, quant and finance**: a take-home, a paired
  coding session, or a case study. Ask at the first screen what the process involves so
  you can plan.
- **Directness is expected.** Singapore interviewers tend to ask closed, factual questions
  and expect specific answers. Long preamble reads as evasive.

### Questions worth asking

- "What does success look like in the first six months?"
- "Is this a new role or a replacement?" — the answer changes the risk profile.
- "How is the team structured across the region?" — Singapore roles are often regional-HQ
  roles, and the answer tells you whether the scope is local or APAC.
- For a role needing a pass: "Has this role been advertised on MyCareersFuture, and is the
  salary band able to support an Employment Pass application?" This is a legitimate,
  professional question and saves a wasted process.

### Offer-stage items

- **CPF** applies to citizens and PRs only; employer contribution is ~17% on top of gross
  for most of the band. It is not part of the quoted salary, so compare offers on gross
  base plus AWS plus variable bonus, and note CPF separately.
- **AWS / 13th month** is common but not guaranteed. Confirm whether it is contractual.
- **Variable bonus** is usually stated as months of base. Ask what the last two payouts
  actually were — the stated target and the paid reality often differ.
- **Leave**: 14–18 days annual leave is typical for experienced hires; statutory minimum is 7.

## Common Tough Questions

### "Why did you leave [previous company]?"
> The APAC region was restructured in May 2026 and my role was made redundant. It was a
> structural decision, not a performance one — I had been running the region's sales
> operations for nine years. I used the timing deliberately: I enrolled in the SMU Academy
> generative AI certificate and have been studying full-time since. Say this plainly and
> move on. Do not editorialise about the employer, and do not apologise.

### "You don't have [specific skill/experience]."
> For technical asks: "That's right, and it's deliberate. I'm not a programmer. My value is
> knowing which commercial processes are worth changing and having the operating experience
> to prove whether the change worked — I specify and measure, and work with the people who
> build." Then give the 18%-to-7% example, which is a systems change achieved without
> writing code.
>
> For "no experience in our sector": name the transferable structure — six markets, a team
> of 40, a CRM migration, a forecast cadence — and say what differs in their sector. Do not
> claim sector experience he does not have.

### "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?"
> Running commercial operations for a regional business — the same layer I have owned, with
> more scope. The GenAI work is what makes that different from the last nine years: I want to
> be the person who can tell a leadership team which commercial processes are worth
> automating and prove the result, rather than running a pilot that never lands.

### "What's your biggest weakness?"
> Technical depth. I can read a dashboard and challenge a number, but I cannot build the
> pipeline behind it. I have chosen to go deep on the commercial side instead, and I work
> with analysts rather than pretending to be one. It has been the right trade for 24 years;
> it is the wrong trade if a role needs me to be the engineer.

### "Why this company specifically?"
> Customize per company. Must reference: specific projects, company values, market position, or team structure. Never give a generic answer.

## Questions You Should Ask Interviewers

### About the Role
- "What does a typical week look like in this role?"
- "What would success look like in the first 6 months?"
- "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"

### About the Team
- "How big is the team, and how do you divide work?"
- "What does the development/project lifecycle look like, from idea to production?"
- "How do you onboard new team members?"

### About Tech & Growth
- "What's your current tech stack for [relevant area]?"
- "Is there room to grow into more architectural or strategic decisions?"
- "How does the team stay current with new tools and methods?"

### About Culture (use these to prevent disappointment)
- "How would you describe the team culture?"
- "What does professional development look like here?"
- "Is there flexibility for remote/hybrid work?"
- "What's the balance between development/new projects and maintenance work?"
- "How would you describe the leadership style in this team?"
- "What do people who thrive here have in common?"

## Phone/Video Interview Tips
- Have STAR examples written out (use this file)
- Keep a glass of water nearby
- Smile when speaking (it changes your tone)
- Ask for clarification if a question is vague
- It's OK to take 5 seconds to think before answering
- End with: "Is there anything else you'd like to know about my background?"

## After the Application (Best Practice)

### Follow-Up Etiquette
- **Don't call to "stand out"** or to learn more about the role post-submission - this risks a negative impression
- If the employer specified a timeline, respect it and wait
- If no timeline was given and significant time has passed (2+ weeks), a brief call to ask about status is acceptable
- If you have genuinely new, relevant information to share, a short follow-up is fine

### Thank-You Notes
- When you receive any update (interview invitation, rejection, or status update), send a brief thank-you message
- Express appreciation for their time and the process
- Keep it short (2-3 sentences)

## Roleplay Guidelines
When the user asks for interview practice:
1. Ask which role/company to simulate
2. Start with easy warm-up questions ("Tell me about yourself")
3. Progress to role-specific technical questions
4. Include 1-2 behavioral questions using the competencies from the job posting
5. End with a tough question or curveball
6. After each answer, give brief feedback: what worked, what to sharpen
7. Suggest which STAR example would work best for each question
